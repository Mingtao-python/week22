Chemistry: Atomic Structure and Bonding

Chapter 1: Atomic Structure

1.1 Basic Particle Composition
All matter is composed of atoms. An atom consists of:

Nucleus (at the center):
- Protons: Positively charged particles, mass ≈ 1 amu
- Neutrons: Neutral particles, mass ≈ 1 amu

Electron Cloud (surrounding nucleus):
- Electrons: Negatively charged particles, mass ≈ 1/1836 amu
- Located in orbitals at various energy levels

1.2 Atomic Numbers and Mass Numbers
Atomic Number (Z): The number of protons in an atom
- Defines the element
- For neutral atoms: Number of electrons = Number of protons

Mass Number (A): The total number of protons and neutrons
- A = Z + number of neutrons
- Isotopes are atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons

Example: Carbon-12 (¹²C)
- Atomic number = 6 (6 protons)
- Mass number = 12 (6 protons + 6 neutrons)
- Neutral atom has 6 electrons

1.3 Electron Configuration
Electrons occupy orbitals in different energy levels.

Bohr Model (simplified):
- First shell (n=1): Maximum 2 electrons
- Second shell (n=2): Maximum 8 electrons
- Third shell (n=3): Maximum 18 electrons

The Aufbau Principle: Fill lower energy orbitals first before higher ones.

Order of orbital filling: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p...

Notation: s, p, d, f orbitals (s=2e⁻, p=6e⁻, d=10e⁻, f=14e⁻)

Examples:
- Hydrogen (H): 1s¹
- Carbon (C): 1s² 2s² 2p²
- Oxygen (O): 1s² 2s² 2p⁴
- Sodium (Na): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹
- Iron (Fe): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶ or [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶

1.4 Valence Electrons
Valence electrons are the outermost electrons involved in chemical bonding.

Electrons in the outermost s and p orbitals are typically considered valence electrons.

Group trends (periodic table):
- Group 1 (alkali metals): 1 valence electron
- Group 2 (alkaline earth metals): 2 valence electrons
- Group 13-18: 3-8 valence electrons
- Transition metals: Complex, but usually 2 valence electrons from s orbital + d electrons

Chapter 2: Chemical Bonding

2.1 Ionic Bonding
Ionic bonds form when electrons are transferred from one atom to another.

Characteristics:
- Forms between metals and nonmetals
- Metal loses electrons (forms cation with positive charge)
- Nonmetal gains electrons (forms anion with negative charge)
- Electrostatic attraction holds the ions together
- Typically forms crystalline solids
- Conducts electricity in molten or aqueous solution

Examples:
- Sodium chloride (NaCl): Na⁺ transfers 1 electron to Cl⁻
- Magnesium oxide (MgO): Mg²⁺ transfers 2 electrons to O²⁻
- Calcium fluoride (CaF₂): Ca²⁺ transfers 1 electron to each of 2 F⁻ ions

2.2 Covalent Bonding
Covalent bonds form when electrons are shared between atoms.

Types:
Single bond: Sharing of 1 pair of electrons (σ bond)
- H-H, C-H, O-H

Double bond: Sharing of 2 pairs of electrons (1 σ bond + 1 π bond)
- C=C, C=O, N=N

Triple bond: Sharing of 3 pairs of electrons (1 σ bond + 2 π bonds)
- C≡N, N≡N

Polar vs. Nonpolar:
- Nonpolar: Equal sharing of electrons (same atoms or similar electronegativity)
  Examples: H₂, Cl₂, CCl₄
- Polar: Unequal sharing of electrons (different electronegativity)
  Examples: HCl, H₂O, NH₃

Lewis Structures:
Dot notation showing valence electrons and bonds
Example: H₂O has 8 valence electrons total
    H-O-H (with 2 lone pairs on O)

2.3 Metallic Bonding
Metal atoms lose valence electrons to form a delocalized electron "sea."

Characteristics:
- Found in pure metals and alloys
- Atoms arranged in lattice with mobile electrons
- Explains electrical and thermal conductivity
- Explains malleability and ductility

2.4 Electronegativity and Polarity
Electronegativity: Ability of an atom to attract electrons in a chemical bond
Pauling scale: 0.7 (least) to 4.0 (most)

Elements arranged by electronegativity (most common):
Fluorine (4.0) > Oxygen (3.44) > Nitrogen (3.0) > Chlorine (3.16) > 
Carbon (2.55) > Hydrogen (2.20) > Sodium (0.93)

Bond polarity increases with electronegativity difference:
- ΔEN < 0.4: Nonpolar covalent
- 0.4 < ΔEN < 1.7: Polar covalent
- ΔEN > 1.7: Ionic

Chapter 3: Molecular Geometry

3.1 VSEPR Theory
Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory predicts molecular geometry.

Electron pairs (both bonding and lone pairs) repel each other and arrange to minimize repulsion.

2 electron pairs: Linear (180°)
3 electron pairs: Trigonal planar (120°)
4 electron pairs: Tetrahedral (109.5°)
5 electron pairs: Trigonal bipyramidal (90°, 120°)
6 electron pairs: Octahedral (90°)

Examples:
- CO₂: Linear (2 double bonds, no lone pairs on C)
- H₂O: Bent (2 bonds, 2 lone pairs on O) - angle ≈ 104.5°
- NH₃: Trigonal pyramidal (3 bonds, 1 lone pair on N) - angle ≈ 107°
- CH₄: Tetrahedral (4 bonds, no lone pairs on C) - angle = 109.5°

3.2 Hybridization
Atoms can combine atomic orbitals to form hybrid orbitals for bonding.

sp hybridization: 2 hybrid orbitals, linear
sp² hybridization: 3 hybrid orbitals, trigonal planar
sp³ hybridization: 4 hybrid orbitals, tetrahedral

Chapter 4: Periodic Trends

4.1 Atomic Radius
Generally decreases from left to right across a period (more protons attract electrons closer)
Generally increases down a group (more electron shells)

4.2 Ionization Energy
Energy required to remove an electron from an atom
Generally increases from left to right across a period
Generally decreases down a group

4.3 Electron Affinity
Energy change when an electron is added to an atom
Generally increases from left to right (more positive nuclei attract electrons)
Generally decreases down a group (outer electrons farther from nucleus)

4.4 Electronegativity
Generally increases from left to right across a period
Generally decreases down a group
